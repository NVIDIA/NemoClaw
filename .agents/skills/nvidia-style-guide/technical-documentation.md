## Purpose

This writing guide should be referenced when writing technical documentation.
A writing guide is a document that provides guidelines for creating technical content.
The purpose of a writing guide is to ensure that multiple contributors write clearly and cohesively, reflecting the corporate style.

The primary objective of this guide is to ensure writers are creating clear, concise documentation for the following purposes:

- Describing the use, operation, and design of our software through the use of user and developer documentation, APIs, diagrams, and graphics.
- Providing help while using our comprehensive applications.
- Creating documentation to educate customers.

## Writing Guide Versus Style Guide

### What's the Difference?

In technical communication, the terms “writing guide” and “style guide” are often used interchangeably; however, a writing guide encompasses various aspects of effective writing, such as:

- Grammar rules
- Syntax (sentence structure)
- Clarity and conciseness
- Organization and flow of ideas
- Audience analysis and adaptation
- Developing a strong argument or narrative

A style guide tends to be more specific, focusing on consistent application of certain conventions within a particular context, such as:

- **Formatting:** How titles, headings, citations, and references should be structured.
- **Punctuation:** Rules for using commas, dashes, quotation marks, and so on, that might differ based on the specific style guide (for example, Oxford comma usage).
- **Capitalization:** Guidelines for capitalizing words in titles, headings, and throughout the text.
- **Spelling:** Preference for US or UK English, specific capitalization of terms related to a brand or organization.
- **Brand Voice and Tone:** Defining the personality and attitude conveyed through writing for a specific organization or brand.

## The Writing Process

*(Transcribed from a diagram on the page: a hand writing with a pen, labeled with the stages of writing.)*

- **Before Writing:** Plan and Investigate
- **Main Ideas:** Related, Focused
- **Write:** Clear, Concise
- **Review:** Coherence, Grammar

To be a successful technical writer, it is essential to understand your purpose and audience when writing.
Your audience is the person (or people) who will read what you write, and your purpose is your reason for writing.
It is essential to understand your audience before beginning the writing process, so you can determine the type of content to produce (for example, a user guide, developer guide, or quick start guide) to achieve your intended purpose.

Determining the audience and the purpose are just the first two steps in the technical writing process.
Creating and adhering to a process enables the creation of high-quality technical writing quickly and efficiently.
When writing, it is important to perform the following actions:

### 1. Understand Your Audience

Who are you writing to?
How much does your audience already know about the subject?
Is the reader familiar with the terms and jargon you may need to use?
What tone should you adopt?
What does your reader need to know in order for you to fulfill your purpose in writing?
What does your reader need to know in order to have enough information to do what you want them to do?

### 2. Determine Your Purpose

You must clearly know your purpose before you can start.
Why are you writing?
What is your aim or goal?
What do you intend to accomplish in these release notes, user documentation, or developer documentation?
What do you want your reader to know or to do as a result of your writing?

### 3. Brainstorm Your Ideas

With your purpose and audience clearly in mind, brainstorm, and write down all ideas related to the subject.
Include facts and information as well as notes about writing style and approach and even vocabulary.
Without limiting or rejecting anything (yet), list everything you can think of related to the subject, your purpose, and your audience.

### 4. Choose and Sort Your Ideas

Choose the best ideas that you have, and eliminate anything that will not help you to fulfill your purpose.
Sort your ideas into categories.
Ensure you have everything you need to accomplish your purpose.

### 5. Organize Your Ideas into a Writing Plan

Create your writing plan.
Organize and order your ideas using lists, clustering, classification trees, graphs, or charts to create a clear and logical structure.
Eliminate anything that does not directly relate to your purpose.
Where should new paragraphs start?
Should you use headings, lists, charts, or graphics?
Do you have a specific writing format that you follow to help organize your writing?
Review your writing plan.
Will it fulfill your purpose?
Are you missing any steps?
Are you missing any important information?

### 6. Write the First Draft

Follow your plan and just write.
Don't worry about grammar, word choice, or spelling.
Keep your audience and purpose in mind.

### 7. Revise, Correct, and Rewrite

First, ensure that your writing fulfills its purpose.
Is anything else needed?
Could anything be taken out?
If you think your reader may have any questions, answer them.
Then, review your writing to ensure it is clear and easy to understand.
Is it cohesive and easy to follow?
Are headings and topic sentences appropriate?
Next, ensure your writing exhibits both effective paragraph structure and coherent sentence structure.
Do you have a variety of sentence types?

Finally, proofread and edit your writing for any grammar issues and word-choice errors.

### 8. Send a “Clean” Draft to Your Reviewers

When incorporating technical content, a technical writer is only as good as their subject matter experts (SME).
If the documentation is not thoroughly reviewed, the final version can contain gaps and incorrect information.
It is essential that reviewers carefully review the documentation.

### 9. Write and Publish the Finished Copy

Incorporate the feedback for your reviews, ensure you conduct a final check for accuracy, and have the SME review it one last time before publishing.

## Writing for Your Audience

Along with the guidelines in [The Writing Process](#the-writing-process), it is important to perform the following actions when writing for your audience:

### 1. Make Sure Your Grammar Is Accurate

Like most foreign language training, English for Speakers of Other Languages (ESOL) teaching focuses heavily on grammar.
In fact, it is not unusual to find that ESOL students have a better grasp of grammar than many US graduates, so if your writing is grammatically incorrect, it will be confusing or misleading for your readers.
They may read grammatical mistakes as deliberate (and confusing) choices, and they will lack the familiarity with common language patterns to work out what you meant.

### 2. Use Short Sentences

The longer the sentence, the more likely it is that your reader will lose the gist of it.
Long sentences tend to have more complex structures and dependent clauses, which can make the main point difficult to discern.
They will also look intimidating to non-native speakers.
If you have long sentences, try splitting them up so that each is no more than 35 words.

When writing, brevity can help with comprehension and localization.
The following are some tips to help tame long sentences:

- Avoid redundancy
- Avoid flowery language
- Avoid padding
- Write in active voice
- Use an authoritative voice

#### Avoid Redundancy

Redundancy consists of unnecessary repetition and gets in the way, taking up unnecessary space, so we do not have as much room to develop our documentation and convey things that actually matter.
Redundancy also makes it difficult to understand our point because the reader has to work harder to figure out what we’re getting at.

- *Example:* The process of freeing a **stuck** vehicle **that has been stuck**…
- *Correction:* The process of freeing a stuck vehicle consists of…

#### Avoid Flowery Language

Flowery language, which can be another form of redundancy, is a very elaborate way of writing.
Writing is considered flowery if it uses too many complicated words or phrases in an attempt to sound skillful.

- *Example:* High-quality learning environments are a necessary precondition for the facilitation and enhancement of the ongoing learning process.
- *Correction:* People need good schools to learn properly.

#### Avoid Padding

Padding words and phrases are things like “in my opinion” or “as a matter of fact,” which make a sentence longer but do not usually add much meaning.

Saying “In my opinion, the political atmosphere is toxic,” for instance, means exactly the same as “The political atmosphere is toxic.”
If you need to shorten a sentence, try looking for padding phrases you could remove.

#### Write in Active Voice (When Possible)

The two grammatical voices are *active* and *passive*.
*Active* voice means that a sentence has a subject that acts upon its verb.
*Passive* voice means that a subject is a recipient of a verb’s action.
When the subject of a sentence performs the verb’s action, the sentence is in the active voice.
Sentences in the active voice have a strong, direct, and clear tone.
The following are a couple of brief and straightforward examples of active voice versus passive voice.

- Active: Marti logged into the account. (Correct)
- Passive: The account was logged into by Marti. (Incorrect)

In technical communication, active voice illustrates authority and confidence; however, there might be times when using active voice alters the intended meaning of the sentence, as shown in the following:

- Passive voice: The man was bitten. (Correct)
- Active voice: The man bit. (Incorrect)

To rewrite the passive voice sentence correctly in the active voice, you need to know by what or whom the man was bitten.

Note: Whenever possible, use active voice because it provides a more explicit meaning.
Also, try to avoid using *intransitive verbs*, which do not take a direct object.
In other words, it is not done to someone or something; it only involves the subject.

#### Use an Authoritative Voice

Writing in an authoritative voice and using direct statements doesn’t mean that you’re bossy, rude, or condescending.
It means that you’re confident and knowledgeable about your subject.
It also means that you can convey that confidence in your writing style by avoiding redundancy, flowery language, and padding.
Additionally, keep in mind that humor and colloquial language do not always translate well.

### 3. Limit Abstract Nouns

An abstract noun is a word that refers to an event, state, quality, concept, or feeling: essentially, it is a thing that does not exist physically.
For example, meeting, consideration, anger, freedom, research, problem, and so on.
You will not be able to get rid of them completely, but try not to have too many in each sentence.

- *Before:* The outcome of the meeting was an agreement to commission research into the subject of Spanish culture to allow for predictions of potential problems.
- *After:* During the meeting, we agreed to research Spanish culture so we can predict any possible problems.

By turning many abstract nouns into verbs (predictions to predict) and rewording to eliminate others, you get a more manageable and much shorter sentence.

### 4. Be Careful with Modal (or Helping) Verbs

The primary helping or modal verbs are “shall” and “should,” “will” and “would,” “may” and “might,” “can” and “could,” and “must.”
They show how the main verb is meant to be read and are used very naturally by native speakers.
Consider the difference between being told you “could” finish your report by the end of the day (where the main verb is “finish”) or that you “must” finish it by the end of the day.
They are not as clear-cut as they first seem, particularly for global readers.
Look out for unclear sentiments.

### 5. Avoid Negatives

Questions phrased in the negative, such as “You do not have the research results, do you?” are harder to follow and could provoke the opposite response than was intended out of confusion.
Be straightforward: “Do you have the research results?”

Double negatives are also problematic.
In English, they equal a positive; in some languages, such as Spanish, they merely emphasize the negative aspect.
We read “not unlikely” as “likely,” but a Spanish reader, for example, would probably understand it as “very unlikely.”
In some cultures, such as Japanese, the word “no” is habitually avoided.
Others do not even have a translatable version of it.
Yet others view negative language as insulting.
As with any writing, it is vital to know your audience before you begin.

### 6. Avoid Colloquial Language and Idioms

A common suggestion for translators and interpreters is to avoid using colloquial language, such as slang and idioms.
There is often confusion between these two types of informal writing.
An idiom is a phrase that holds a particular meaning with only a specific group of people, while a colloquialism is a word or phrase that is considered informal.
Idioms often include clichés, and colloquialisms include slang.
An easy way to identify whether something is an idiom or not is to read the words out of context and determine whether they still hold the same meaning.

When writing, the most important rule you should always ask yourself is, “What do these words mean to my audience?”
With that said, avoid using terms and idioms that are ubiquitous solely to your geography or language.

## Voice and Tone

As writers, we want to communicate in a friendly, straightforward way.
We should consider our voice to be:

- Authoritative
- Instructive
- Welcoming to all audiences

### Authoritative

Writing in an authoritative voice doesn’t mean that you’re bossy, rude, or condescending.
It means that you’re confident and knowledgeable about your subject.
It also means that you can convey that confidence in your writing style.
To do that, we need to choose our words wisely to:

- [Avoid redundancy](#avoid-redundancy)
- [Avoid flowery language](#avoid-flowery-language)
- [Write in active voice](#write-in-active-voice-when-possible)
- [Choose the right level of formality](#formality)

### Formality

The level of formality should be determined by the audience's expectations and the purpose.
Whenever possible, use the **second person** and use the pronoun “you.”
When discussing the product's functionality using second-person, write prose that empowers the user to take action.

*Note:* Do not write, “The product allows you to create multiple user segments.”
Instead, write, “With the product, you can create multiple user segments.”

### Instructive

Many people resist reading instructions.
They try to figure out for themselves how to operate a product or perform a task and will turn to the instructions only if all their efforts fail.
When they do read the instructions, they want to understand everything immediately without having to reread anything.
A simple design, clear wording, and concise instructions will be crucial in encouraging readers to follow your instructions or procedures.

When writing technical documents and instructions, there are several style tips you should keep in mind:

- Use a lot of declarative, command, or direct address kinds of writing. It is OK to use “you” when writing instructions because you are addressing the reader directly.
- Use active instead of passive voice.
- Do not leave out articles such as a, an, and the.
- Use action verbs.
- Do not use emojis, as they are not suitable for technical documentation.
- Ensure graphics (such as screenshots and diagrams) match descriptive text to avoid confusion.
- Keep the text short but descriptive.
- Avoid complicated jargon; use simple verbiage to ensure understanding by a broad spectrum of users.
- Use concise headings and subheadings to describe and highlight each section.
- Leave plenty of white space around headings.
- Highlight safety information and warnings.
- Keep illustrations as simple as possible.
- Do not use exclamation points that aren't a part of code samples.

### Declarative vs. Imperative Sentences

A sentence can be categorized into four types based on its functions.
These four categories are known as *declarative*, *imperative*, *interrogative*, and *exclamatory*.
Two types commonly used in technical communication are *declarative* and *imperative*:

- *Declarative* sentences are sentences that relay information, opinions, and facts. This is the most common sentence type used in the language, and they are punctuated with a full stop.
- *Imperative* sentences are sentences that act as commands, demands, and orders. Their primary function is issuing commands and requests. Imperative sentences are typically short; they can be as short as one word. Also, they can be punctuated with a full stop or an exclamation mark.

The differences between declarative and imperative sentences are shown in the following table:

| Declarative | Imperative |
| --- | --- |
| Relays information, opinions, and facts | Issues commands and requests |
| Ends with a full stop | Ends with a full stop or exclamation mark |
| Sentences are at least two words long | Sentences can consist of one verb |
| Sentences consist of a subject and a predicate (complete action) | Sentences do not explicitly state a subject |
| Sentences can have more than one subject | Always has or references the second person pronoun |

*Declarative examples:*

- The dogs barked at the moon.
- Jim worked hard, but he failed the exam.
- She performed for hours; the audience went wild.

*Imperative examples:*

- Close the door.
- Come here, look at this dress, and tell me what you think.
- Stop!

Note: When writing technical documentation, declarative language is used most frequently; however, elements of imperative language can be acceptable when appropriate.

### Welcoming to All Audiences

We’re always working to make our content more accessible and usable to the widest possible audience.
As you write, consider the following:

- Would this language make sense to someone who doesn’t work here?
- Could someone quickly scan this document and understand the material?
- If someone can’t see the colors, images, or video, is the message still clear?
- Is the markup clean and structured?
- Mobile devices with accessibility features are increasingly becoming core communication tools. Does this work well on them?

### Capitalization Rules

At first glance, the rules of English capitalization seem simple.
You probably know you should capitalize proper nouns and the first word of every sentence, but you also (sometimes) capitalize the first word of a quote.
Usually, you don’t capitalize after a colon, but there are exceptions, and what do you do when you’re not sure whether something is a proper noun?
Knowing when or when not to capitalize a word can become tricky.

Here are some important rules regarding capitalization:

#### Capitalize the First Word of a Sentence

This one’s easy. Always capitalize the first word of a sentence.

- The cat is sleeping.
- Where did I put that book?
- Hey! It’s great to see you! How have you been?

#### Capitalize Names and Other Proper Nouns

You should always capitalize people’s names.

- My favorite author is Jane Austen.
- Tom and Diane met at Judy’s house.
- Have you met my dog, Boomer?

Names are proper nouns.
The names of cities, countries, companies, religions, and political parties are also proper nouns, so you should capitalize them too.

- We experienced some beautiful Southern California weather last fall when we attended a Catholic wedding in San Diego.

You should also capitalize words like *mom* and *grandpa* when they are used as a form of address.

- Just wait until Mom sees this!
- *But:* My mom is not going to like this.

#### Don’t Capitalize After a Colon (Usually)

In most cases, you don’t need to capitalize after a colon.

- I have one true passion: horse racing.

There are a couple of common exceptions.
One is when the word following the colon is a proper noun.

- There is only one place I want to visit: New York City.

The other exception is when the words following the colon form one or more complete sentences.

- Maggie wears a brimmed cap at all times for these two reasons: Strong light often gives her a headache. She also likes the way it looks.

#### Capitalize the First Word of a Quote (Sometimes)

Capitalize the first word of a quote when the quote is a complete sentence.

- Mario asked, “What is everyone doing this weekend?”
- Stacy answered, “My sister and I are going to the water park.”

Don’t capitalize the first word of partial quotes.

- Gretchen said she was “way too busy” to join the gym.
- Mr. Thompson described the rules as “extremely difficult to understand if you don’t have a law degree.”

#### Capitalize Days, Months, and Holidays, but Not Seasons

The names of days, months, and holidays are proper nouns, so you should capitalize them.

- I hate Mondays!
- Tom’s birthday is in June.
- Oh no! I forgot about Valentine’s Day!

The names of seasons, however, are not proper nouns, so there’s no need to capitalize them.

- I hate winter!
- Having a summer birthday is the best.

#### Capitalize Most Words in Titles

The capitalization rules for titles of books, movies, and other works vary a little between style guides.
In general, you should capitalize the first word, all nouns, all verbs (even short ones, like *is*), all adjectives, and all proper nouns.
That means you should lowercase articles, conjunctions, and prepositions—however, some style guides say to capitalize conjunctions and prepositions that are longer than five letters.

- *Sense and Sensibility* is better than *Pride and Prejudice*.
- The first movie of the series is *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone*.

#### Capitalize Cities, Countries, Nationalities, and Languages

The names of cities, countries, nationalities, and languages are proper nouns, so you capitalize them.

- English is made up of many languages, including Latin, German, and French.
- My mother is American, and my father is of Creole descent.
- The capital of Botswana is Gaborone.

#### Capitalize Time Periods and Events (Sometimes)

Specific periods, eras, and historical events that have proper names should be capitalized.

- Most of the World War I veterans are now deceased.
- In the Middle Ages, poor hygiene was partly responsible for the spreading of bubonic plague.
- Middle school students often enjoy studying the social changes that took place during the Roaring Twenties.

However, centuries—and the numbers before them—are not capitalized.

- In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, England blossomed into an empire.

### Relative Pronouns

#### What Are They?

Relative pronouns such as “which” and “that” are words that begin adjective clauses.
Often, relative pronouns can be removed from a sentence without changing its meaning.
In fact, at times, the omission of these clauses results in cleaner, tighter sentences.
For clarity, it is essential to know which one to use and when.

Clauses can be essential because they can provide the reader with more information about the noun they follow.
Clauses that start with “which” are *nonrestrictive* or *nonessential* clauses.
A nonrestrictive/nonessential clause is a clause that does not limit the essential meaning of the element it modifies, and if it were removed from the sentence, the implied meaning behind it would remain the same.
Nonrestrictive/nonessential clauses should always be set off from the rest of the sentence with commas.

*Example:* La Sagrada Familia, which is one of my favorite historical sites, is in dire need of continued renovation.

In this case, you could drop the clause “which is one of my favorite historical sites,” and the sentence would retain its meaning.

Clauses that begin with “that” are called *restrictive* because they provide information *only* about the noun being discussed.
A restrictive clause is essential to the meaning of the sentence element it modifies.
In other words, without this clause, the sentence would not have the same meaning.
Unlike nonrestrictive/nonessential clauses, restrictive clauses should not be set off by commas.

*Example:* The cat that lived around the corner came to my house every morning.

#### Which One to Use?

A simplified grammar rule states, “If your sentence has a clause but does not need it, use ‘which’; if the sentence does need the clause, use ‘that.’”
To provide more context, determining the use of “which” or “that” depends on the specific meaning you want to convey in your sentence.

*Example:*

- The building, which towered over the tourists, gave me the creeps.
- The building that towered over the tourist gave me the creeps.

In the first sentence, the fact the building towered over the tourists bothered me, while the second sentence implies the building itself bothered me.

Often, sentences do not require the use of any clauses and read much better without their use.
In technical documentation, we tend to overuse phrases like “that is/are.”

*Example:*

- Insert the disk in the drive that is located under the left panel.
- Insert the disk in the drive located under the left panel.

Both sentences have the same implied meaning, but the second sentence is a little tighter.
While editing your work, run a global check for these phrases, and I guarantee you can omit the majority of them.

### Punctuation Placement and Quotation Marks

If you were educated outside of the United States, it is understandable that the placement of your commas and periods might be on the outside of quotation marks.
In North America, the following is the proper placement for commas, periods, colons, semicolons, question marks, and exclamation points.

#### Commas and Periods

Put commas and periods **within** quotation marks, except when a parenthetical reference follows or it is a part of a code string.

*Examples:*

- He said, “I may forget your name, but I never forget a face.”
- History is stained with blood spilled in the name of “civilization.”
- Mullen, criticizing the apparent inaction, writes, “Donahue’s policy was to do nothing” (24).
- In Java, a string is a sequence of characters. For example, “hello” is a string containing a sequence of characters ‘h’, ‘e’, ‘l’, ‘l’, and ‘o’.

#### Colons and Semicolons

Place colons and semicolons **outside** closed quotation marks.

*Examples:*

- Biles emphasizes three elements of what she calls her “Olympic journey”: family support, personal commitment, and great coaching.
- Williams described the experiment as “a definitive step forward”; however, other scientists disagreed.

#### Question Marks and Exclamation Points

Place a question mark or exclamation point **within** closing quotation marks if the punctuation applies to the quotation itself.
Put the punctuation **outside** the closing quotation marks if the punctuation applies to the whole sentence.

*Examples:*

- “Do you need this book?”
- Professor Z always tells her students, “You know I do not accept late work, right”?

### Dashes

When using dashes, it is essential to use the correct one because they serve different purposes.
For em dash and en dash rules, refer to [Dashes and hyphens](SKILL.md#dashes-and-hyphens--) in SKILL.md; in technical documentation, use commas or parentheses instead of em dashes.

To type the dashes:

- **Em dash (—):** Alt+0151 on Windows; Option+Shift+Hyphen (-) on macOS
- **En dash (–):** Alt+0150 on Windows; Option+Hyphen (-) on macOS

#### Hyphens

Hyphenate two or more words that precede and modify a noun as a unit if confusion might result without the hyphen.

*Examples:*

- built-in drive
- high-level-language compiler
- read-only memory
- lower-left corner
- floating-point decimal
- line-by-line
- up-to-date information

Also, hyphenate if one of the words is a past or present participle (a verb form ending in -ed or -ing and used as an adjective or noun).

*Examples:*

- left-aligned text
- free-flowing form
- well-defined schema

### Periods and Bulleted Lists

Using punctuation, namely periods, in bulleted lists can be very confusing—especially if you use *The Chicago Manual of Style* for guidance, as it has a large section on bullets and punctuation.
Hopefully, this helps simplify its rules a bit:

#### When Periods Are Required

- Use a period (full stop) after every bullet point that is a sentence.
- Use a period after every bullet point that completes the introductory stem.
- Use no punctuation after bullets that are not sentences and do not complete the stem.
- Use all sentences or all fragments, not a mixture.

The following is an example of bullet points that complete the introductory stem.

> I like living in the Bay Area because of its:
>
> - Access to culture, natural beauty, and work opportunities.
> - Moderate climate–not too hot or too cold.
> - Liberal politics and social attitudes.

#### When Periods Are Not Required

The following is another version that does not need periods.

> Here are the things I like about living in the Bay Area:
>
> - Access to culture, natural beauty, and work opportunities
> - Moderate climate–not too hot or too cold
> - Liberal politics and social attitudes

#### The Exception to the Rule

There is an exception to putting periods after bullet points that complete the stem sentence.
If they are one word or a short phrase that feels like an inventory or shopping list, do not use end punctuation, such as in the following example.

> I like living in the Bay Area because of its:
>
> - Culture
> - Natural beauty
> - Work opportunities
> - Moderate climate
> - Liberal politics
> - Social openness

While we don't (often) use vertical lists punctuated as a sentence in technical writing, it is my responsibility to:

> - provide you with an example to illustrate this format;
> - although I find it very clunky,
> - you might need to do this one day.

### Semicolons

A semicolon is a punctuation mark (;) indicating a pause, typically between two main clauses, that is more pronounced than that indicated by a comma.
The semicolon is often misused as it has only three (3) uses:

- Connecting related independent clauses
- Creating a serial list
- Linking two independent clauses with a conjunctive adverb

**IMPORTANT:** Semicolons should be used sparingly in technical documentation because they can cause unnecessary sentence complexity.
Before using a semicolon, consider simplifying your sentences and using periods for complete sentences.

#### Semicolons Connect Related Independent Clauses

You can use a semicolon to join two closely related independent clauses.
Let’s put that another way.
The group of words that comes before the semicolon should form a complete sentence, and the group of words that comes after the semicolon should form a complete sentence.
The two sentences should share a close, logical connection:

- I ordered a cheeseburger for lunch; life’s too short for counting calories.
- Money is the root of all evil; I don’t believe the reverse is necessarily true.
- Martha has gone to the library; Andrew has gone to play soccer.

Notice that the letter following the semicolon is not capitalized.
The examples above are both made up of two complete, grammatically correct sentences glued together.
Yes, that means there are six total sentences up there—and thanks to the semicolon, only two capital letters.
That’s precisely why you can’t substitute a comma for a semicolon.
Using a comma instead of a semicolon in the sentences above would result in a comma splice.

#### Use Semicolons in a Serial List

You can use semicolons to divide the items of a list if they are long or contain internal punctuation.
In these cases, the semicolon helps readers keep track of the divisions between the items.

- I need the weather statistics for the following cities: London, England; London, Ontario; Paris, France; Paris, Ontario; Perth, Scotland; Perth, Ontario.
- My plan included taking him to a nice—though not necessarily expensive—dinner; going to the park to look at the stars, which, by the way, are amazing this time of year; and serenading him with my accordion.

Let’s recap: so far, we’ve got semicolons for linking two independent clauses; replacing a conjunction (whether showing similarity, like “and,” or opposition, like “but”); and long, comma-loving lists.

#### Use Semicolons with Conjunctive Adverbs

When you have a conjunctive adverb linking two independent clauses, you should use a semicolon.
Some common conjunctive adverbs include *moreover, nevertheless, however, otherwise, therefore, then, finally, likewise,* and *consequently.*

- I needed to go for a walk and get some fresh air; subsequently, I needed to buy milk.
- Reports of the damage caused by the hurricane were greatly exaggerated; indeed, the storm was not a “hurricane” at all.
- The students had been advised against walking alone at night; however, Cathy decided walking wasn’t dangerous if it was early in the evening.
- I’m not all that fond of the colors of tiger lilies; moreover, they don’t smell very good.

These words sometimes show up in other parts of a sentence; therefore, the semicolon rule only applies if it helps the conjunctive adverb join two independent clauses.
This conjunctive adverb rule is similar to the conjunction rule.
In both cases, check that the two ideas are independent clauses that could stand on their own as sentences.
If so, then you’re grammatically good to go as far as the semicolon is concerned.
